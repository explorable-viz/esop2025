\begin{abstract}
   Scientific claims are routinely justified and explained using visual summaries computed by data analysis
   and visualisation code. Fact-checking or understanding such claims requires understanding how the charts
   and figures relate to the data they purport to be about, and can be difficult and time-consuming even for
   an expert. Recent work has shown how a dynamic program slicing technique called \emph{Galois slicing} can
   be used to transparently link visualisations and other outputs to data, so a reader can explore
   fine-grained input-output relationships by interacting directly with computed artefacts. Galois connections
   provide a precise characterisation of these relationships in terms of demand and sufficiency. Most recently
   \citeauthor{perera22} extended this framework to support the exploration of output-output relationships,
   i.e.~outputs that are indirectly related in virtue of demanding some of the same inputs. However,
   implementations of these techniques to date have relied on forwards and backward re-execution via a
   sequential execution record, or \emph{trace}, making them too slow to be suitable for interactive use.

   In this paper we present a new implementation technique for Galois slicing based on dynamic dependence
   graphs, which decomposes the analysis into two steps: an operational semantics that evaluates a program to
   a dependence graph, and then a pair of forwards and backwards slicing operators defined over the graph. We
   show that the one-off overhead of producing the dependence graph is comparable to that of producing a
   trace, but that the slicing operators run on average much faster than their trace-based counterparts on the
   interactive scenarios. As well as being faster, the graphical approach is also simpler; for example forward
   slicing over a dependence graph is simply the De Morgan dual of backward slicing over the opposite graph,
   leading to a naive implementation which although slower than our direct forward slicing algorithm is still
   faster than the trace-based approach. Moreover, our slicing algorithms are language-agnostic and the Galois
   connections are determined solely by the reachability relation $R$ between the inputs and outputs of the
   graph. We are able to recover the ``related outputs'' operator of \citeauthor{perera22} via the Galois
   connection for $R \relcomp R^{-1}$ and also to derive a new \emph{related inputs} operator from the Galois
   connection for $R^{-1} \relcomp R$ with its own novel data visualisation applications.
\end{abstract}
