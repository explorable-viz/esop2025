\begin{abstract}
   Scientific claims are routinely justified and explained using visual summaries --- charts and figures ---
   computed using data analysis and visualisation libraries. Understanding how these visual outputs relate to
   the data they purport to be about is a challenge facing anyone trying to evaluate or understand such a
   claim. Recent work has used techniques from dynamic program slicing to link visualisations and other
   outputs automatically to the underlying data, allowing a reader to explore the fine-grained bidirectional
   relationships between data and visualisation by interacting directly with the output. Galois connections
   have proved to be an effective formal foundation for these approaches, characterising the relationship
   between outputs and inputs in terms of demand and sufficiency; most recently \citeauthor{perera22} extended
   this ``Galois slicing'' approach to allow a user to explore how outputs are related in the sense of
   demanding overlapping inputs. However, implementations of these approaches to date have relied on a
   sequential execution record, or \emph{trace}, to wind the computation forwards and backwards. This reliance
   on forwards and backward re-execution has meant that the analyses are too slow for the intended interactive
   use cases.

   In this paper we present a new implementation technique for Galois slicing based on dynamic dependence
   graphs which decomposes the analysis into two steps: an operational semantics that evaluates a program to a
   dependence graph, and then a pair of adjoint operators for forwards and backwards slicing over the graph.
   We show that the one-off overhead of producing the dependence graph is comparable to that of producing a
   trace, but that the slicing algorithms which support the interactive scenarios run on average much faster
   than their trace-based counterparts, making them fit for the intended purpose. We also show that,
   extensionally, forward slicing over a dependence graph is the De Morgan dual of backward slicing over the
   opposite graph, leading to an easy implementation of the upper adjoint in terms of the lower. Even this
   naive implementation is on average faster than the trace-based approach, but we also give an even faster
   direct implementation. In the graphical approach, the slicing algorithms are language-agnostic and the
   mathematical setup is simpler, as the Galois connections are determined solely by the reachability relation
   $R$ between the inputs and outputs of the graph. The competing outputs operator from earlier work is given
   by the Galois connection for $R \relcomp R^{-1}$, and a new \emph{cooperating inputs} operator is given by
   $R^{-1} \relcomp R$ which also has interesting data visualisation applications.
\end{abstract}
