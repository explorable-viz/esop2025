\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our work is motivated by the difficulty and time consuming nature of producing
interactive data visualizations. Visualizations provide a valuable mechanism for
communicating scientific findings and exploratory data analysis, but implementations
are often bespoke for a single visualization. By shifting the burden into a language
runtime, time and effort can be saved by a visualizations developer, who now only
need to express the data transformations they wish to perform and the charts they
will use to communicate their results. Prior work aimed at integrating these
features with language runtimes suffered due to a reliance on bi-directional
program evaluation and complex semantics. Decomposing the approach by realizing
data dependencies into a dependence graph and then applying analyses over that
graph has the advantage of simplifying the semantics needed to represent data
dependencies, and easing the burden on implementers, without negatively impacting
the performance of interactions in most cases.

\subsection{Future work}

The analysis algorithms presented in \secref{conjugate} are neutral with respect to any intensional information
associated with the dependence graph, in the sense that any relationships they discover are determined only
by the \emph{extension} -- I/O relation -- of the graph, rather than any internal paths. It follows that
transformations that elide internal nodes but preserve I/O connectivity, such as the classical Y-$\Delta$
transform \cite{truemper89}, will produce an extensionally equivalent (but faster) analysis. Although we leave
applications of this observation to future work, we conjecture that it might be used to selectively trade
intensional information for additional performance improvements.

The fact that our work is neutral with respect to intensional information in the dependence graph raises
the question of what information the internal nodes represent. The choice of dependency relation
we represent in the graph is the same as that of \citet{perera22}; here, selections are pushed through
evaluation if the parts of the values they select are consumed during evaluation - either via
pattern matching or the specific implementation provided for a primitive.

One question raised by this is how the structure of the dependence graph would change if a different
dependency relation were to be implemented. This question suggests a formal treatment of the choice of
dependency relation $R$ and the information it would allow us to compute about internal nodes in the graph.
For example, \citet{cheney11b} considers the use of dependency analysis to compute \textit{expression
provenance} information about elements of a program. One use of this information is to synthesize a
sub-expression describing the derivation of a value at a given point. We can ask the question of how to apply
this at the level of the dependence graph. For example it is natural to ask: can we associate the internal
nodes of the dependence graph with an  \textit{expression} that describes the provenance information of that
node.
