\section{Introduction: Towards Transparent Research Outputs}
\label{sec:introduction}

Whether formulating a national policy or simply deciding what groceries to buy, we increasingly rely on the
charts, figures and text created by scientists and journalists. Interpreting these visual and textual
summaries is essential to making informed decisions. However, most of the artefacts we encounter are
\emph{opaque}: they are unable to reveal anything about how they relate to the data they were derived from.
Whilst one could in principle try to use the source code and data sources to reverse engineer some of these
relationships, this requires substantial expertise, as well as valuable time spent away from the
``comprehension context'' in which we encountered the output in question.  These difficulties are only
compounded when the information presented draws on multiple data sources, such as medical
meta-analyses~\citep{dersimonian86}, ensemble models in climate science~\citep{murphy04}, or queries that span multiple database
tables~\citep{selinger79}. Even professional reviewers may lack the resources or inclination to embark on such
an activity. Perhaps more often than we would like, we end up taking things on trust.

With traditional print media, a ``disconnect'' between outputs and underlying data is unavoidable. For digital
media, other options are open to us. One way to improve things is to engineer visual artefacts to be more
``self-explanatory'' so they can reveal the relationship to the underlying data to an interested user.
Consider the histogram in \figref{introduction:bremer} showing urban population growth in Asia over a 10-year
period \cite{bremer15}. There are many questions a reader might have about what this chart represents --- in
other words, how visual elements map to underlying data. Do the points represent individual cities? What does
the colour scheme indicate? Which of the points represent large cities or small cities? Legends and other
accompanying text can help, but some ambiguities inevitably remain. The entire value proposition of a summary
after all is that it presents the big picture at the expense of certain detail.

\citeauthor{bremer15} implemented an interactive feature that allow a user to explore some of these questions
themselves \emph{in situ}, i.e., directly from the chart. By selecting an individual point, they are able to
bring up a view showing the data that point was calculated from. In this case the highlighted dot represents
data for Chiang Mai and the plotted value of 10.9\% was derived from an increase in population density from
4,416 to 5,010 people per sq.~km.

\subsection{Data transparency as PL infrastructure}

Features like these are valuable comprehension aids but are laborious to implement by hand, as done in \cite{bremer15}. They also require
the author to anticipate the queries a user might have and do not readily generalise: for example
\citeauthor{bremer15}'s visualisation only allows the user to select one point at a time.

For these reasons, there has been recent interest in treating data transparency and exploration as a programming language infrastructure
problem, baking dependency metadata directly into outputs so that interactive queries of this sort can be
supported automatically. \edit{The author of the content no longer has to concern themselves with these tricky
features, and in fact may find that they have an easier time exploring data, and building more complex models.
Similarly, the consumer of the content now has the best of both worlds:} the ability to explore detail on an
as-needed basis in the context of the high-level perspective offered by the visual summary. The relationship
to data can be incrementally discoverable, revealed as the user interacts with the parts of the output they
care about, ideally with some kind of formal guarantee that the revealed data is in some sense ``minimal and
sufficient''. 

In data visualisation it has long been recognised that when users interact with visual elements by clicking or
lassoing, the intention is typically to manipulate the underlying data. This has lead to various techniques
for inverting selections in visual space to obtain selections in the input data space~\cite{north00, heer08}.
\edit{\citet{psallidas18smoke} propose techniques based on fine-grained data provenance; for a custom
in-memory database engine, they describe how to ``backward trace'' from output selections to input
selections, and then ``forward trace'' to find related output selections in other views, to support a popular
feature from data visualisation called \emph{linked brushing}. Moving forward, we refer to this feature
as \emph{linked outputs} instead. \citet{perera22} take this further, applying the idea
of forwards and backards tracing to a general purpose functional language as opposed to a language for
data visualization. Their work lets them ``rewind'' a program and trace fine-grained dependency information from outputs back to inputs.}

Next, we use the example in Fig.~\ref{fig:introduction:related-inputs} to describe the concept we are concerned with in this paper, namely that of fine-grained in situ
provenance queries, which we call \emph{data transparency}.
For context, let us imagine that we are a data scientist investigating the efficiency of clean energy usage, broken down geographically.
The data is split across the two tables dedicated to \textit{renewable} and \textit{non-renewable} energy sources, respectively.
The data from the tables has been aggregated into a view, which is our case is represented by the scatter plot on the right hand side of the figure, where the x-axis represents the total renewables capacity over the total energy capacity, whereas the y-axis denotes the total green energy output over the total green energy capacity.
Below, a user may refer to either an observer that can only see the output views, or a developer that has access to both the input data and the output views.
Also, we sometimes refer to the input data as simply ``inputs'', and the corresponding views as ``outputs''. 
There are four scenarios we focus on to illustrate data transparency:

\paragraph{Fine-grained linking of the view back to the data}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:introduction:related-inputs}, the dots in the scatter plot do not provide any details about the data they were computed from, which may be of interest to a user.
For instance, when selecting the dot highlighted in green, a user may want to identify all the values from the two tables that were used to compute its x and y coordinates (this data is highlighted in grey in the figure).

This is particularly opaque as the plot doesn't make it possible to understand what green energy even refers to. In our design, by selecting the y-coordinate of the dot corresponding to China, the user can see exactly the considered data. \paragraph{Linked outputs}
The previous use cases only went in one direction, either from outputs back to inputs, or from inputs to outputs. However, there may be situations where we want to compose the two directions.
One such scenario is illustrated in \figref{overview:scatterplot}, where we have two output views, the same scatter plot as in Fig.~\ref{fig:introduction:related-inputs}, and an additional bar chart
denoting the non-renewables by country. Let's assume that a user selects \cd{fix and describe selection} in the bar chart. As previously explained, this will highlight the corresponding
data in the nonRenewables table that was used to produce those outputs. Additionally, it will also highlight the outputs in the scatter plot that were produced by the same data. 
We refer to the selected bars in the bar chart and the selected points in the scatter plot as ``linked outputs'', as they are generated from the same data \cd{compete for the same input data?}. 

From the analysis point of view, this requires applying $\demandsR$ to find the value used to produce the selected segment of the bar, with each set of coloured segments
associated with a given nation -- the selected segments demand all of  the highlighted green data in the table.
Then, starting from the selected data, we apply $\demandedByR$ to compute the $x$ components of some of the circles in the scatterplot (scatterplot, green circles).

\paragraph{Linked inputs}
Dually, we can start from an input, and want to determine all the ``linked inputs'', i.e. inputs that contribute to the same features of an output. This enables
users to formulate queries like “What outputs use this data element, and what other data elements are used along with it?”.
For illustration, if we go back to Fig.~\ref{fig:introduction:related-inputs}, and start by selecting China's bioenergy capacity, then, as explained above,
our system will apply $\demandedByR$ to trace the data contained in this cell to the point in the chart it was used to compute (the green dot in the plot).
Subsequently, it will apply $\demandsR$ to determine the rest of the data in the input tables that was used to compute the point in the point in the chart (highlighted in grey).

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction:contributions}

We introduce a new program analysis framework which supports
interactive exploration of fine-grained I/O relationships, as those described in the previous section.
Our approach is language-independent, %which means that changes to the underlying language will not affect the analysis (?).
using \emph{dynamic dependence graphs}~\citet{ferrante87, agrawal90}. This separates the definition of
queries over the I/O dependencies for a particular program from the problem of generating a dependence graph
for that program, which enables:

\begin{itemize}

\item Easily updating the language, as well as adding new analyses. With respect to the latter, we are the first to propose the linked inputs analysis, a relation of mutual relevance or “cognacy” which arises between inputs when they contribute to common features of the output. The design of our framework makes it straightforward to implement this analysis by composing the available operands.

\item Improved performance with respect to previous works. As shown in the experimental evaluation, \edit{$81\%$ of the queries we test over larger examples execute at a speed that appears instantaneous to a user, compared to $25\%$ using an implementation based on traces}. Fast response time is particularly important in the context of real-time systems\edit{, such as the interactive data visualizations we target}.

\item Correctness checks that  take advantage of the duality of the proposed operators. For this purpose, we use the notions of conjugacy~\citet{jonsson51} and Galois connection \cd{add citation}.

These contributions are not available in previous works. Hand-crafted efforts like \citet{bremer15}'s are not
only labour-intensive, but involve manually embedding metadata about the relationship between visual outputs
and inputs into the same program. The validity of this metadata is fragile: whenever the visualisation or data
analysis logic changes, the relationships between inputs and outputs also change. By shifting the
responsibility for gathering this information into the programming language, the author or data scientist can
concern themselves purely with analysis and visualisation, and defer the provision of these transparency
features to the infrastructure used to implement and host the visualisation.

However, computing the bidirectional dependency information needed to realise this idea is costly both in terms
of performance and implementation burden.
For example, the approach taken by \citet{perera22} utilised a bidirectional interpreter that builds an execution
record in the form of a \emph{trace} during a forwards execution,
folding that trace back into a program slice during a backwards
execution. The two directions of the interpreter need to be kept
synchronised, which makes updating the language difficult as every
change requires modifying each direction in a manner that maintains
bidirectionality.  Every analysis implemented in their framework needs
to be defined separately and depends on details of the language,
imposing a significant burden on the implementor.  Moreover each runs
over an execution record or trace of the entire computation regardless
of whether all of the trace is relevant.

\paragraph{Roadmap} \secref{overview} presents an overview of our approach and language, and motivates the idea of ``related
inputs'' queries from an end-user perspective. The rest of the paper is organised as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \secref{conjugate} defines a new program analysis framework over dynamic dependence graphs, introducing
  the cognacy operator $\relInputR$ (``linked inputs'') and unpacking the intuitive relationship between its
  two components $\demandsR$ (``demands'') and $\demandedByR$ (``demanded by'') in terms of
  \citeauthor{jonsson51}'s notion of conjugate operators over Boolean algebras. We also introduce the dual
  cognacy operator $\relOutputR$ (``linked outputs'') and explain the relationship to Galois connections: $f$
  and $g$ being conjugate is equivalent to $f$ and the De Morgan dual of $g$ forming a Galois connection. We
  give procedures for computing $\demandsR_{R}$ and $\sufficesR_{R}$ over a directed graph with reachability
  relation $R$.
\item \secref{core} defines a core functional language with an operational semantics that pairs every result
  with a dynamic dependence graph suitable for computing $\demandsR$ and $\demandedByR$. We show how to represent
  (parts of) values as vertices in the graph. \edit{We also show how our operators can follow information back to environments,
  with projections.}
\item \secref{evaluation} compares the performance of our implementation based on graphs and conjugates with an
  implementation due to \citet{perera22} based on traces and Galois connections, contrasting the overhead of
  building trace vs.~building dependence graphs, computing $\demandsR$ and $\demandedByR$ over each, and the
  relative implementation burden of the two approaches. 
\item \secref{related-work} reviews related work in more detail, including program slicing and DDGs.
\item \secref{conclusion} wraps up with a discussion of some limitations. In particular, the sort of transparency
infrastructure proposed in this paper is purely extensional and falls short of providing full explanations of
how output parts are related to input parts. We discuss extending this to more ``intensional'' forms
transparency and propose some other ways in which the present system could be improved.

\end{itemize}
