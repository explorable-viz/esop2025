\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A ubiquitous problem in data-driven decision making --- from setting national policy to deciding what
groceries to buy --- is interpreting the charts, figures and text created by scientists and journalists. These
sorts of data summaries are essential to making and supporting claims, but lose crucial information about the
relationship to the underlying data, making them hard to fact-check or drill into. If we know the domain well
and have access to the source code and data sets used to create them, we may be able to reverse engineer some
of these relationships, but this is unlikely to be easy. In many common situations we have little choice but
to take things on trust. These difficulties are further compounded when the information presented draws on
multiple data sources, such medical meta-analyses ~\citep{nakamura19}, ensemble models~\citep{virdee23}, or
queries that span multiple database tables~\citep{selinger79}.

With traditional print media, opaque outputs disconnected from the underlying data are to be expected. For
digital media, other options are open to us. In this paper, we introduce a new program analysis framework
which supports interactive exploration of input-output relationships directly via visual outputs such as
charts or tables. While this idea is not entirely new (see for example \citet{psallidas18} and
\citet{perera22}), our framework lends itself to easier implementation than prior work and supports a novel
form of provenance query which we call \emph{related inputs}. Related inputs characterises the relation of
mutual relevance which arises between two different input elements when they contribute to a common output
element; to discover such relationships, we make use of \emph{dynamic dependence graphs} (DDGs), an approach
used in the program slicing literature for analysing imperative programs~\cite{ferrante87}. We formalise
related inputs as the image function for $R^{-1} \relcomp R$ where $R$ is the reachability relation for a
dependence graph $G$, and give a procedure for computing related inputs over the graph. Borrowing a term from
linguistics, we say that inputs related in this way are \emph{cognate} because their relatedness amounts to
having a common ancestor in $G$ (or rather its opposite $G^{-1}$, but we will use cognacy to refer to common
ancestry in either). The key mathematical idea is the concept of ``conjugate'' operators~\cite{jonsson51},
functions between Boolean algebras which come in reciprocating pairs and which when ``self-composed''
calculate relations of cognacy.

Decomposing the problem into a graph-building semantics and a pair of analyses over the graph also
significantly reduces the implementation burden on the developer, since the slicing algorithms are
language-agnostic and thus only need to be implemented once. The mathematical setup is also simpler in this
new setting, because the conjugate operators are determined solely by the reachability relation $D$ between
the inputs and outputs of the graph.

\subsection{Contributions}

The specific contributions of the paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \secref{example} uses a realistic example to introduce the novel cognacy operator $\relInput$ (``related
inputs'') along with its components $\demandR$ and $\demandByR$. We unpack the intuitive relationship between
$\demandR$ (``demands'') and $\demandByR$ (``demanded by'') in terms of \citeauthor{jonsson51}'s notion of
conjugate operators and also introduce the flipped composite $\relOutput$ (``related outputs'') that computes
the dual cognacy relation.
\item \secref{core} defines the core functional language underlying our implementation and gives an
operational semantics that pairs every result with a DDG suitable for computing $\demandR$ and $\demandByR$.
\item \secref{conjugate} introduces the formal idea of conjugate operators between Boolean algebras and show
that for a relation $D \subseteq X \times Y$, the image and preimage functions for $D$ form a conjugate pair
$\demandByR_{D}$ and $\demandR_{D}$ between $\powerset{X}$ and $\powerset{Y}$. We show that $f$ and $g$ are
conjugate is equivalent $f$ and $\dual{g}$ being adjoint.
\item \secref{graph} defines two procedures for computing $\demandR_{D}$ and $\demandByR_{D}$ over a DDG with
reachability relation $D$.
\item \secref{evaluation} compares the performance of our implementation based on graphs and conjugates with
an implementation due to \citet{perera22} based on traces and Galois connections. We contrast the overhead of
building a trace vs.~ building a DDG, computing $\demandR$ and $\demandByR$ over both, and the relative
complexity of the two implementations.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \secref{related-work} discusses related work in more detail, and \secref{conclusion} concludes with
a discussion on some limitations of the present system and how it might be developed further in the future.
