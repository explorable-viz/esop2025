\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A ubiquitous problem in data-driven decision making --- from setting national policy to deciding what
groceries to buy --- is interpreting the charts, figures and text presented to us by scientists and
journalists. When data is summarised in this way, important ideas can be communicated efficiently, but equally
crucial information about how those summaries were constructed, and from what data, is lost. If we know the
domain well and have access to the source code and data sets, we may be able to reconstruct some of this lost
information, although that will usually take a significant investment of time. More commonly we take things on
trust. These difficulties are further compounded when the output presents information collated from multiple
sources, such medical meta-analyses ~\citep{nakamura19}, ensemble models~\citep{virdee23}, and database
joins~\citep{selinger79}.

With traditional print media, opacity in this sense is probably unavoidable, but we should be able to do
better with online digital media. In this paper, we introduce a new program analysis framework which supports
interactive exploration of input-output relationships directly via online computed artefacts such as charts or
tables of data. While this idea is not entirely new (see for example \citet{psallidas18} and
\citet{perera22}), our framework lends itself to easier implementation than prior work and supports a novel
form of provenance query which we call \emph{related inputs}. Related inputs characterises the relation of
mutual relevance which arises between two different input elements when they contribute to a common output
element; to discover such relationships, we make use of \emph{dynamic dependence graphs} (DDGs), an approach
used in the program slicing literature for analysing imperative programs~\cite{ferrante87}. We formalise
related inputs as the image function for $R^{-1} \relcomp R$ where $R$ is the reachability relation for a
dependence graph $G$, and give a procedure for computing related inputs over the graph. To borrow a term from
linguistics, we say that inputs related in this way are \emph{cognate} because their relatedness amounts to
having a common ancestor in $G$ (or rather its opposite $G^{-1}$, but we will use cognacy to refer to common
ancestry in either). The key mathematical idea is the concept of ``conjugate'' operators on Boolean
algebras~\cite{jonsson51}, which describes functions between Boolean algebras which can be ``flipped'' into
functions running in the other direction and then ``self-composed'' to calculate relations of cognacy.

Our approach decomposes Galois slicing into two components: an operational semantics that
evaluates a program to a DDG, and a pair of adjoint slicing operators for forwards and backwards slicing over
the graph. We show that although the overhead of producing a DDG is comparable to that of producing a trace,
the slicing algorithms run on average much faster than their trace-based counterparts, making them suitable
for interactive applcations like brushing and linking. Moreover, although bckward slicing is the usual
reachability analysis over the graph, in turns out that forward slicing over a dependence graph is,
extensionally, the De Morgan dual of backward slicing on the opposite graph, leading to an easy implementation
of the upper adjoint in terms of the lower. This naive implementation is still faster than the trace-based
approach, but we also give a direct implementation of forward slicing and show that it performs even better.

Decomposing the problem into a graph-building semantics and a pair of analyses over the graph also
significantly reduces the implementation burden on the developer, since the slicing algorithms are
language-agnostic and thus only need to be implemented once. The mathematical setup is also simpler in this
new setting, because the Galois connections are determined solely by the reachability relation $R$ between the
inputs and outputs of the graph. This allow the ``competing outputs'' problem formulated in \citet{perera22}
to be understood as the Galois connection for $R \relcomp {R^{-1}}$, which in turn suggests another Galois
connection for ``cooperating inputs'' given by $R^{-1} \relcomp R$. We show how this new operator also has potentially interesting data science applications.

\subsection{Contributions}

The specific contributions of the paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \secref{example} introduces the novel cognacy operator $\relInput$ (``related inputs'') with an example.
We unpack the intuition in terms of \citeauthor{jonsson51}'s notion of conjugate operators and also introduce
the dual operator $\relOutput$ (``related outputs'').
\item \secref{core} defines the core functional language that is the basis of our implementation and gives an
operational semantics that pairs every result with a DDG suitable for computing $\demandR$ and $\demandByR$.
\item \secref{graph} introduces the idea of a Galois connection for a relation, formalises DDGs as one
possible ``implementation'' of such a relation, and defines adjoint forwards and backwards slicing operators
over a DDG which implement the corresponding a Galois connection.
\item \secref{evaluation} contrasts the graph-based approach with a trace-based implementation, comparing the
overhead of building a trace vs.~a DDG, slicing over both, and the relative ease of implementing the graphical
approach.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \secref{related-work} discusses related work in more detail, and \secref{conclusion} concludes with
a discussion on limitations and future work.
