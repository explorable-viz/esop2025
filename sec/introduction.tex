\section{Introduction: Towards Transparent Research Outputs}
\label{sec:introduction}

Whether formulating a national policy or simply deciding what groceries to buy, we increasingly rely on the
charts, figures and text created by scientists and journalists. Interpreting these visual and textual
summaries is essential to making informed decisions. However, most of the artefacts we encounter are
\emph{opaque}: they are unable to reveal anything about how they relate to the data they were derived from.
Whilst one could in principle try to use the source code and data sources to reverse engineer some of these
relationships, this requires substantial expertise, as well as valuable time spent away from the
``comprehension context'' in which we encountered the output in question.  These difficulties are only
compounded when the information presented draws on multiple data sources, such as medical
meta-analyses~\citep{dersimonian86}, ensemble models in climate science~\citep{murphy04}, or queries that span multiple database
tables~\citep{selinger79}. Even professional reviewers may lack the resources or inclination to embark on such
an activity. Perhaps more often than we would like, we end up taking things on trust.

With traditional print media, a ``disconnect'' between outputs and underlying data is unavoidable. For digital
media, other options are open to us. One way to improve things is to engineer visual artefacts to be more
``self-explanatory'' so they can reveal the relationship to the underlying data to an interested user.
Consider the histogram in \figref{introduction:bremer} showing urban population growth in Asia over a 10-year
period \cite{bremer15}. There are many questions a reader might have about what this chart represents --- in
other words, how visual elements map to underlying data. Do the points represent individual cities? What does
the colour scheme indicate? Which of the points represent large cities or small cities? Legends and other
accompanying text can help, but some ambiguities inevitably remain. The entire value proposition of a summary
after all is that it presents the big picture at the expense of certain detail.

\citeauthor{bremer15} implemented an interactive feature that allow a user to explore some of these questions
themselves \emph{in situ}, i.e., directly from the chart. By selecting an individual point, they are able to
bring up a view showing the data that point was calculated from. In this case the highlighted dot represents
data for Chiang Mai and the plotted value of 10.9\% was derived from an increase in population density from
4,416 to 5,010 people per sq.~km.

\subsection{Data transparency as PL infrastructure}
\label{sec:introduction:data-transparency}

Features like these are valuable comprehension aids but are laborious to implement by hand, as done by
\citet{bremer15}. Manual implementations also require the author to anticipate the queries a user might have
and do not always generalise well: for example \citeauthor{bremer15}'s visualisation only allows the user to
select one point at a time.

For these reasons, there has been recent interest in treating data
%transparency and
exploration as a programming language infrastructure
problem, baking dependency metadata directly into outputs so that interactive queries of this sort can be
supported automatically~\cite{ricciotti17,perera22}. The author of the content no longer has to concern themselves with these tricky
features, and in fact may find that they have an easier time exploring data, and building more complex models.
Similarly, the consumer of the content now has the ability to explore detail on an
as-needed basis in the context of the high-level perspective offered by the visual summary. In contrast to \citet{bremer15}, the relationship
to data can be incrementally discoverable, revealed as the user interacts with the parts of the output they
care about, ideally with some kind of formal guarantee that the revealed data is in some sense ``minimal and
sufficient''.

We follow the direction of~\citet{ricciotti17} and \citet{perera22}, and propose a new bidirectional program analysis framework which supports
such fine-grained \emph{in situ} provenance queries, which we call \emph{data transparency}. Next, we use
the example in~\figref{introduction:related-inputs} to describe this concept.
For context{, the figure presents a simple piece of data visualisation, investigating} the efficiency of clean
energy usage, broken down geographically. The data is split across two tables dedicated to \textit{renewable}
and \textit{non-renewable} energy sources; the data from the tables has been aggregated into a view, namely
the scatter plot on the right hand side of the figure, where the x-axis represents the total renewables
capacity over total energy capacity, and the y-axis denotes the clean capacity factor computed as the total clean energy output over the total clean energy~capacity.

There are four scenarios below we focus on to motivate the idea of data transparency as an end-user feature. When we say ``user'', this may refer to either a reader

\paragraph{Fine-grained linking of the view back to the data}
In~\figref{introduction:related-inputs}, the dots in the scatter plot do not provide any details about the data they were computed from, which may be of interest to a user.
For instance, when selecting the dot highlighted in green, a user may want to identify all the values from the two tables that were used to compute its x and y coordinates (this is denoted by all the highlighted data in the two tables).
%\paragraph{Fine-grained linking of the view to the data}
Furthermore, they may be interested in more fine-grained information, e.g. they may only want to know about
the clean energy, reflected in the plot by the y-axis. This is particularly opaque as the plot doesn't make it
possible to understand what ``clean'' energy even refers to.
By selecting %supporting fine-grained in situ provenance
%queries, in our design, the user can select only
the y-coordinate (visible when clicking the dot), the user
will be presented
with a set of highlighted cells in the input tables whose contents were used to produce the selected output (all the highlighted cells in renewables, and the cells corresponding to the nuclearCap and nuclearOut columns on row 52 in nonRenewables). Notably, this clarifies that nuclear energy is considered clean energy.

As we will see later in the paper, our system uses an operator $\demandsR_{G}$, which identifies the complete set of input data that was used to compute the selected output.
%(\figref{introduction:related-inputs}, grey selections).
We read this operator as computing ``what input data does an output \emph{demand}?''.
The subscript $G$ is short for ``graph'', denoting that the computation is based on the reachability relation of a dependence graph $G$.

\paragraph{Fine-grained linking of the data to the view}
A user may also be interested in following the link between the input data and the output view. For instance,
the user may select China's bioenergy capacity (highlighted in green in~\figref{introduction:related-inputs}, row 205 in the data on the figure's left).
Then, our system, through an operator we write as $\demandedByR_{G}$, traces the data contained in this cell to
the parts of the plot it was used to compute (the green dot in the plot). The bioenergy capacity
contributes to both components of the dot, namely the total renewables capacity over total energy
capacity and the clean capacity factor. We read $\demandedByR_{G}$ as computing
``what outputs is some input data \emph{demanded by}?''.

\paragraph{Linked inputs}
The previous use cases only went in one direction, either from outputs back to inputs, or from inputs to
outputs. However, there may be situations where we want to compose the two directions.
For illustration, let's assume like before that a user starts by selecting China's bioenergy capacity.
As previously explained,
our system will apply $\demandedByR_{G}$ to trace the data contained in this cell to the point in the scatter plot it was used to compute (the green dot in the plot).
%The bioenergy capacity contributes to both components of the dot, namely the total renewables capacity over total energy capacity and the clean capacity factor.
%\MNnote{I'm not sure if it should be obvious to me that it contributes to both components, or if this is simply something I'm  being told is true.}
Additionally, our system will apply $\demandsR_{G}$ to determine the rest of the data in the input tables that was used to compute the point in the plot (all data highlighted in grey in the tables).
We refer to the highlighted data in the two input tables as ``linked inputs'', i.e., inputs that contribute to the same features of an output. This enables
users to formulate queries like: “which outputs use this data element, and which other data elements are used along with it?”.


\paragraph{Linked outputs}
Dually, we can start from an output, and want to determine all the
“linked outputs”, i.e., outputs that compete for the same input data.
One such scenario is illustrated in \figref{introduction:scatterplot}
where we have two output views: the same scatter plot as in
\figref{introduction:related-inputs}, and an additional bar chart
denoting the non-renewables by country.  Let's assume that a user
selects the segments corresponding to India in 2017, and Egypt
  and Japan in 2018 in the bar chart. This will highlight the
corresponding data in the \textsf{nonRenewables} table that was used to produce
those outputs. Additionally, it will also highlight any outputs in the
scatter plot that demand any of those data. The selected bars in the
bar chart and the selected points (or, more specifically, their x-ccordinates as those are the ones using the considered input data) in the scatter plot are the linked
outputs. In the scatter plot, we note that only two dots are selected,
those for Egypt and Japan, reflecting the fact that the scatter plot
is only a view of data from 2018.

From the analysis point of view, this requires applying $\demandsR_{G}$ to find the value used to produce the selected segment of the bar, with each set of coloured segments
associated with a given nation -- the selected segments demand all of the highlighted green data in the table.
Then, starting from the selected data, we apply $\demandedByR_{G}$ to pick out the $x$ components of some of the circles in the scatter plot (green circles).

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction:contributions}

We introduce a new bidirectional program analysis framework which supports interactive exploration of
fine-grained I/O relationships, as described in the previous section. We make use of dynamic dependence
graphs~\cite{ferrante87, agrawal90}; this enables our approach to be language-independent, separating the
definition of queries over the dependence graph for a particular program, from the problem of deriving the
dependence graph for that program. Our specific contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}

\item A new formal framework for bidirectional provenance queries over dependence graphs, formalising the
$\demandsR_{G}$ and $\demandedByR_{G}$ operators introduced informally in \secref{introduction:data-transparency},
which we show to be \emph{conjugate} in the sense of~\citet{jonsson51}. We relate our framework to
bidirectional analyses based on Galois connections (e.g.~\citet{ricciotti17,perera22}).

\item A novel \emph{linked inputs} analysis, a relation of mutual relevance or “cognacy” which arises between
inputs when they contribute to common features of the output. The design of our framework makes it
straightforward to implement this analysis by composing $\demandsR_{G}$ and $\demandedByR_{G}$. We also show how a dual
\emph{linked outputs} analysis, available in prior work \cite{perera22}, is easily obtained by transposing the two operators.
%  \MNnote{I suggest taking this part out, because we don't actually do this:}, and relate this formally to the ``linked brushing'' feature from the data visualisation literature.

\item Improved performance with respect to \emph{trace-based} approaches~\cite{perera22}, one of the predominant techniques for achieving bidirectional program analysis.
  Using usability metrics from \citet{nielsen93}, we find that $81\%$ of the queries we tested
over larger examples execute at a speed that appears instantaneous to a user, compared to $25\%$ using an
implementation based on traces. Fast response time is particularly important for the interactive data
visualisations we~target.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Roadmap} The  paper is organised as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \secref{overview} presents an informal overview of our approach and language.
\item \secref{conjugate} defines a new program analysis framework over dynamic dependence graphs, introducing
  the cognacy operator $\relInputR_G$ (``linked inputs'') and unpacking the intuitive relationship between its
  two components $\demandsR_{G}$ (``demands'') and $\demandedByR_{G}$ (``demanded by'') in terms of
  \citeauthor{jonsson51}'s notion of conjugate operators over Boolean algebras. We also introduce the dual
  cognacy operator $\relOutputR_G$ (``linked outputs'') and explain the relationship to Galois connections: $f$
  and $g$ being conjugate is equivalent to $f$ and the De Morgan dual of $g$ forming a Galois connection. We
  give procedures for computing $\demandsR_{G}$ and $\demandedByR_{G}$.
\item \secref{core} defines a core functional language with an operational semantics that pairs every result
  with a dynamic dependence graph suitable for computing $\demandsR_{G}$ and $\demandedByR_{G}$. We show how to represent
  (parts of) values as vertices in the graph. We also show how our operators can follow information back to environments,
  with projections.
\item \secref{evaluation} evaluates our implementation based on graphs and conjugates against one based on traces and Galois connections. We contrast the
 implementation burden of the two approaches, and the performance  of
  building trace vs.~building dependence graphs and computing $\demandsR_{G}$ and $\demandedByR_{G}$ over~each.
\item \secref{related-work} reviews related work in more detail, including program slicing and dynamic dependence~graphs.
\item \secref{conclusion} wraps up with a discussion of some limitations. In particular, the sort of transparency
infrastructure proposed in this paper is purely extensional and falls short of providing full explanations of
how output parts are related to input parts. We discuss extending this to more ``intensional'' forms
transparency and propose some other ways in which the present system could be improved.

\end{itemize}
