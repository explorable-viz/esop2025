\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A ubiquitous problem in data-driven decision making --- from setting national policy to deciding what
groceries to buy --- is interpreting the charts, figures and text created by scientists and journalists. These
sorts of data summaries are essential to making and supporting claims, but lose crucial information about the
relationship to the underlying data, making them hard to fact-check or drill into. If we know the domain well
and have access to the source code and data sets used to create them, we may be able to reverse engineer some
of these relationships, but this is unlikely to be easy. In many common situations we have little choice but
to take things on trust. These difficulties are further compounded when the information presented draws on
multiple data sources, such medical meta-analyses ~\citep{nakamura19}, ensemble models~\citep{virdee23}, or
queries that span multiple database tables~\citep{selinger79}.

With traditional print media, this sort of ``disconnect'' between chart and figures and the underlying data is
to be expected, but for digital media, other options are open to us. In this paper, we introduce a new program
analysis framework which supports interactive exploration of input-output relationships directly via visual
outputs such as charts or tables. While this idea is not entirely new (see for example \citet{psallidas18} for
a discussion on the relationship between data provenance and data visualisation), our framework supports a
novel form of provenance query which we call \emph{related inputs}, as well as lending itself to easier
implementation than prior work. Related inputs characterises the relation of mutual relevance which arises
between two different input elements when they contribute to a common output element. Users are able to ask
questions of the form ``How is this data element used, and what other data elements are used along with it?''
To answer such questions, we make use of \emph{dynamic dependence graphs} (DDGs), an execution record used in
the program slicing literature for analysing imperative programs~\cite{ferrante87}. We formalise related
inputs as the image function for $R^{-1} \relcomp R$ where $R$ is the reachability relation for a dependence
graph $G$, and give a procedure for computing related inputs over $G$. Borrowing a term from linguistics, we
say that inputs related in this way are \emph{cognate} because their relatedness amounts to having a common
ancestor in $G$ (or rather its opposite $G^{-1}$, but we will use cognacy to refer to common ancestry in
either). The key mathematical idea is the concept of ``conjugate'' operators~\cite{jonsson51}, functions
between Boolean algebras which come in reciprocating pairs and which when ``self-composed'' calculate
relations of cognacy.

In their ``reciprocating'' nature, conjugate pairs resemble Galois connections. In fact the relationship is
very close: if $f$ and $g$ are conjugate, then $f$ and the De Morgan dual $\dual{g}$ form a Galois connection.
This relates our approach to earlier work based on Galois connections and execution traces that addressed the
problem of \emph{brushing and linking} in data visualisation~\cite{perera22}. In our framework, the image
function for the flipped composite $R \relcomp R^{-1}$ captures a dual cognacy relation between output
features which compete for common input data; composing this operator with suitable projections at either end
recovers brushing and linking. However, because the core data structure is language-agnostic, our graphical
approach is easier to implement than one based on execution traces. We also demonstrate that it is fast enough
to support the kind of interactive scenarios we have in mind.

\subsection{Contributions}

The specific contributions of the paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item We motivate the novel cognacy operator $\relInput$ (``related inputs'') from an end-user perspective
(\secref{example}). Using a realistic example, we unpack the intuitive relationship between its two components
$\demandR$ (``demands'') and $\demandByR$ (``demanded by'') in terms of \citeauthor{jonsson51}'s notion of
conjugacy and also introduce the dual cognacy operator $\relOutput$ (``related outputs'').
\item We introduce the formal setting of conjugate operators between Boolean algebras (\secref{conjugate}). We
explain how the image and preimage functions for a relation $D \subseteq X \times Y$ form a conjugate pair
$\demandByR_{D}$ and $\demandR_{D}$ between the powersets of $X$ and $Y$ and that $f$ and $g$ being conjugate
is equivalent to $f$ and the De Morgan dual of $g$ forming a Galois connection. We give procedures for
computing $\demandR_{D}$ and $\demandByR_{D}$ over a directed graph with reachability relation $D$.
\item We define a core functional language with an operational semantics that pairs every result with a
dynamic dependence graph suitable for computing $\demandR$ and $\demandByR$ (\secref{core}). We show how to represent (parts) of values as nodes in the graph.
\item We compare the performance of our implementation based on graphs and conjugates with an implementation
due to \citet{perera22} based on traces and Galois connections (\secref{evaluation}). We contrast the overhead
of building trace vs.~building dependence graphs, computing $\demandR$ and $\demandByR$ over each, and the
relative implementation burden of the two approaches.
\end{itemize}
