\section{Conjugate Operators For Program Slicing}
\label{sec:conjugate}

\subsection{Preliminaries}
\begin{definition}[Complement]
   For any set $X$ and any $Y \subseteq X$, write $\compl{Y} \subseteq X$ to denote the \textit{complement}
   of $Y$ with respect to $X$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Image and Preimage]
   For a relation $D \subseteq Y \times X$, and a subset $Y' \subset Y$, we write
   $D[Y'] : \powerset{Y} \to \powerset{X}$ to denote the image of $Y'$ under $D$. For a subset $X' \subseteq X$
   we write $\preim{D}[X'] : \powerset{X} \to \powerset{Y}$ to denote the preimage of $X'$ under $D$.
\end{definition}

We swap the usual order of $X$ and $Y$, when relating them via $D$, since it allows us to read $Y \mathrel{D} X$ as
"outputs $Y$ demand inputs $X$".

For the this section, when we refer to Boolean algebras, we are
usually referring to the powerset lattice.
Therefore we use the set theoretic symbols $\cap$ and $\cup$ for meet and join.

One might expect the functions used to determine \textit{demands} and \textit{demanded by}
to be inverses of each other. It turns out this notion is not quite correct.
We do not consider individual values in the set of inputs
or outputs, and when selecting a set of inputs, we may select an input that is
entirely unused by the program.
If we were to insist our operations were inverse, it would imply that for a set of
inputs to be demanded by a set of outputs, every input in the set should be used,
which is clearly not always the case. We instead relax the requirement from the
two operations being inverse to their being, \textit{conjugate}, in the sense of \citet{jonsson51}:

\begin{definition}[Conjugate Functions]
   For Boolean algebras, $\powerset{X}$, and $\powerset{Y}$, functions $f: \powerset{X} \to \powerset{Y}$
   and $g: \powerset{Y} \to \powerset{X}$, and subsets $X' \subseteq X$ and $Y' \subseteq Y$,
   we say that $f$ and $g$ are \textit{conjugate} if the following holds:
   $$
      f(X') \cap Y' = \bot \iff X' \cap g(Y') = \bot 
   $$
   Or equivalently:
   $$
      f(X') \cap Y' \neq \bot \iff X' \cap g(Y') \neq \bot
   $$
\end{definition}

Straightforwardly, we would expect this condition to hold. If, for some subsets
$X',Y'$, we find that the resources $X'$ is demanded by has an empty intersection
with $Y'$, you would expect that the resources $Y$ demands has an empty intersection
with $X'$. Otherwise, this condition does not seem to be very strong on the surface.

We also expect our operators to be monotonic in their inputs. Conjugate functions
are not only monotonic, but join-preserving, that is $f(X \cup Y) = f(X) \cup f(Y)$. This
makes sense from the perspective of resource demand. The point we have made about
unused inputs and outputs contributes to the property that we cannot necessarily
expect these functions to preserve top. If there are inputs that are unused,
then running demands on the whole output will necessarily miss out all the unused inputs.
Later, we shall see how we can overcome this limitation, and use this to discover
more information about the relationship between inputs and outputs.

Let us assume, for the moment that $f,g$ are placeholders for the demanded by and
demands functions, and that $g \after f (X')$ computes the set of inputs related
to $X'$. If there are elements of $X'$ that are unused, then $g \after f (X') \neq X'$.
Call this subset $X_0 \subset X'$ with $X_0 \notin g \after f(X')$. Then $x \in X_0 \iff D[\set{x}] = \bot$.
If we write $X_1 = (X' \setminus X_0) \subseteq g \after f(X')$, we can also  write
$g \after f (X') = X_1 \disjunion X_2$, and read $X_2$ as the
new inputs we have discovered through our related inputs query. We can safely discard
$X_0$ as the set of unused inputs. If we apply this to the entire set of inputs,
we can read $X_0$ as the set of inputs that are unused through the entire program.
In a data science scenario, this is extremely useful since we often work with datasets
that have a large amount of spurious data.

Once $X_0$ has been thrown away, we are safe to conclude that $g$ (remember $g$ is
demands) applied to the entirety of the output finds the entirety of the input, and
similar for $f$ applied to the entire input.

Conjugate functions also admit products, which we use to focus queries onto
specific contexts:

\begin{lemma}[Products of Conjugates Admit conjugates]
   Let, $f: X \to Y, g: A \to B$ be functions, with $f': Y \to X, g': B \to A$ their conjugates,
   then $\funProd{f}{g}$ and $\funProd{f'}{g'}$ form a conjugate pair.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Preimage and Image of a relation are conjugate]\label{lem:im-preim}
   For a relation $D \subseteq Y \times X$, and subsets $Y' \subseteq Y, X' \subseteq X$,
   $D[\param]$ and $\preim{D}[\param]$ are conjugate.
\end{lemma}

For convenience throughout this section, we make use of the fact that the
preimage of a relation $D$ is equal to the image of its converse. Thus,
alongside \lemref{im-preim}, we make use of the fact that the image 
of a relation is conjugate to the image of its converse.

The authors
of that paper provide the following alternate definition of conjugacy:

\begin{definition}[Computation of Conjugate Function]\label{def:construct-conjugate}
   Given a function $f: \powerset{X} \to \powerset{Y}$, there can exist only one
   conjugate function $g: \powerset{Y} \to \powerset{X}$. If $g$ exists we can
   calculate it using the following:
   $$
      g(Y') = \bigcap_{f(X') \cap Y' = \bot} \padArg{\compl{X}}{1em} \quad \text{for any $X' \in \powerset{X}$}
   $$
\end{definition}

In general, \defref{construct-conjugate} is inefficient, since it requires 
ranging over all possible values $a \in \mathcal{A}$, and computing their
meet with $b$.

\begin{definition}[Galois Connection For A Relation]
   For a relation $D \subseteq Y \times X$, we write $D_{\star} \dashv D^{\star} : \powerset{X} \to \powerset{Y}$
   to mean the image/preimage Galois connection for a function $f : X \to Y$, with the direction of the relation
   flipped, and the functions defined as:
   \begin{itemize}
      \item $D_{\star}(Y') \eqdef D[Y']$
      \item $D^{\star}(X') \eqdef \neg_{\powerset{Y}} \after D^{-1}[X'] \after \neg_{\powerset{X}} = \bigcup_{D^{-1}[Y'] \subseteq X'} \padArg{Y'}{0em} \quad (Y' \subseteq Y)$
   \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Graph]
   A (directed) graph $G=(V,E)$ is a set of nodes $V$, and a set of (directed) edges $E \subseteq V \times V$
\end{definition}
For the rest of this section, we consider graphs where the nodes represent values
in a \OurLang program, and an edge $(\alpha, \alpha')$ exists if the value that
is associated to $\alpha$ \textit{depends} on the value associated to $\alpha'$.
The choice of dependency relation is irrelevant at this stage, we shall return
to this discussion point in \secref{core}. Our work diverges from traditional
approaches to dynamic dependence graphs since we only consider one type of edge,
as opposed to separately defining data and control-flow edges \citet{agrawal90}.

\begin{definition}[Opposite Graph]
   For a graph $G = (V,E)$, define $G^{-1} \eqdef (V,E^{-1})$ to be the opposite graph,
   where $\param^{-1}$ denotes the relational inverse of an edge.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sources and sinks]
   Define:
   \begin{itemize}
      \item $\sources{G} \eqdef \set{\alpha \in \V(G) \mid \inN{G}(\alpha) = \emptyset}$ \hfill (sources)
      \item $\sinks{G} \eqdef \sources{G^{-1}}$ \hfill (sinks)
   \end{itemize}
\end{definition}


When a dependence graph $G$ is constructed, the sources $\sources{G}$ correspond
to the output nodes, and the sinks $\sinks{G}$ correspond to input nodes. In the
opposite graph, this correspondence is flipped.  

\begin{definition}[Reachability]
   For a graph $G=(V,E)$, the \textit{reachability relation} $R_G$ is the \textit{reflexive transitive
   closure} of $E$.
\end{definition}
We implicitly include edges of the form $e = (\alpha, \alpha)$ in the reachability
relation, since values like constants can be copied from the input directly through
to the output. In these cases, the node trivially depends on itself.

For the rest of this section we generally omit the $\param_G$
from this relation, since it is usually obvious to which graph we refer.
When we are considering a graph $G$ and it's opposite $G^{-1}$,
we will abuse the notation for the preimage and image of $R$,
since $R_G^{-1} = R_{G^{-1}}$. We can now define \textit{demands}
and \textit{demanded by} in terms of $R$.

\begin{definition}[Demands and Demanded By]
   For a graph $G = (V,E)$ with inputs $X \subseteq V$ and outputs $Y \subseteq V$, with reachability relation $R_G$, write the following:
   \begin{itemize}
      \item $\demandR_G \eqdef \restrict{R_G}{S(G) \times T(G)}[\param]$
      \item $\demandByR_G = \demandR_{G^{-1}}$
   \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Let us return to the notion of a related input query. The first step is to select
the input of interest, $X' \subseteq \sinks{G}$. We proceed by finding all nodes
in the output that our selected input nodes are demanded by, $\demandByR(X')$.
We calculate this set by the algorithm detailed in \figref{conjugate:reaches}.

   For $G = (V,E)$, define:
   \begin{enumerate}
      \item $\relInput_G \eqdef \demandR_G\cdot\demandByR_G$\hfill (related inputs)

Our framework allows us to not only identify the inputs which are related to each
other, but also allows us to establish relationships from inputs to inputs that
are only contingent on some part of the total output.

Consider the situation where we have two input tables, say $A$ and $B$, which
work together to produce two output charts $C$ and $D$. If both charts use their
respective inputs in different ways, we naturally would like to be able to
decompose these relationships. 

For example, if $A' \subseteq A$ is related to $B' \subseteq B$, by $C$,
and related to $B'' \subseteq B$ by $D$, then running $\relInput(A) = B' \cup B''$.
If we are only interested in the way $A$ and $B$ are related via $C$, we are
able to clamp down demand induced by $D$, sending it to bottom.

\begin{align*}
   \textrm{Meet and Join} \\
   join &: X \to X \times X \\
   join_{bwd}(a,a') &= a \vee a' \\
   join_{fwd}(a)    &= (a, a) \\
   meet &: X \times X \to X \\
   meet_{bwd}(a) &= (a, a) \\
   meet_{fwd}(a, a') &= a \wedge a'
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
   \textrm{Projections/Injections} \\
   inFst &: A \to A \times B & inSnd &: B \to A \times B \\
   inFst_{bwd}(a, -) &= a & inSnd_{bwd}(-, b) &= b \\
   inFst_{fwd}(a) &= (a, \top) & inSnd_{fwd}(b) &= (\top, b)\\
   \dual{inFst} &: A \times B \to A & \dual{inSnd} &: A \times B \to B \\
   \dual{inFst}_{bwd}(a) &= (a, \bot) & \dual{inSnd}_{bwd}(b) &= (\bot, b) \\ 
   \dual{inFst}_{fwd}(a, -) &= a & \dual{inSnd}_{fwd}(-, b) &= b
\end{align*}
Assume $f: A \to C$, $g: A \to D$
\begin{align*}
   \textrm{Products} \\
   \funProd{f}{g} &: A \to C \times D \\
   \funProd{f}{g}_{bwd}(c, d) &= f_{bwd}(c) \vee g_{bwd}(d) \\
   \funProd{f}{g}_{fwd}(a) &= (f_{fwd}(a),g_{fwd}(a)) \\
   \dual{\funProd{f}{g}}&: C \times D \to A \\
   \dual{\funProd{f}{g}}_{bwd}(a) &= (f_{bwd}(a), g_{bwd}(a)) \\
   \dual{\funProd{f}{g}}_{fwd}(c, d) &= f_{fwd}(c) \wedge g_{fwd}(d)
\end{align*}
